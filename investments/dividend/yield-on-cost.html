How this computes

Total return decomposes the way John Bogle framed it: starting yield + earnings growth + change in the multiple. The first two are what you can screen for; the third is the part nobody forecasts honestly. Leaving price growth tracking dividend growth sets that third term to zero — the neutral assumption, and the one worth planning against.

That is why the screening grid reduces to (1 + g) × (1 + y) − 1 and doesn't depend on the horizon. A 3% yield growing at 9% returns about 12.3% a year whether you hold it for eight years or thirty. Time changes the size of the outcome, not the rate.

Yield on cost is the figure this style of investing is really about. It is the current dividend measured against what you originally paid, so it climbs even when the market yield stays flat — and it is why a 3% starter can become a double-digit income stream without the stock ever looking high-yielding to anyone buying it today.

What the model does not know: dividends get cut, payout ratios drift upward before they do, and multiples compress for years at a time. A constant growth rate held for thirty years is an arithmetic exercise, not a forecast. Treat the grid as a screen — the combinations worth researching — rather than as a return you have located.
