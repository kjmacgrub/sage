How these numbers are built

Source: monthly price and full dividend history from Yahoo Finance, split-adjusted, pulled today. Universe is the S&P 1500 — 1,504 current constituents of the S&P 500, MidCap 400 and SmallCap 600. 1,025 have enough dividend history to measure: 399 large cap, 269 mid, 357 small. The rest either pay nothing or have fewer than three complete dividend years.

Small and mid caps change the risk, not just the count. A 30-year dividend record from a $2bn company is a different claim than the same record from a $200bn one — thinner coverage, more concentrated end markets, and far worse option liquidity if you ever want to sell premium against it. The Index column is there so you always know which you are looking at.

The window matters. Dividend growth and price appreciation are both measured from the first full dividend year that the price series also covers, through the last complete calendar year. Measuring dividends over 63 years and price over 40 would flatter one against the other — so both use the same span, shown in the Window column.

Yield is trailing twelve months of dividends over the current price, so a recent raise is already in it. Streak counts consecutive calendar years of higher total dividends; Cuts counts years the annual total fell, across the whole window. A long streak with cuts behind it means the company rebuilt after a reduction — worth knowing before you treat the streak as unbroken.

Yield + Growth is the Chowder number, and it approximates total return CAGR when the multiple is unchanged. Sort on it to see which names carry the arithmetic, then check whether the price CAGR column agrees — a large gap between dividend growth and price growth means the multiple did the work, in one direction or the other.

Payout is trailing dividends over trailing earnings. FCF cover is free cash flow divided by the actual cash cost of the dividend (shares outstanding × trailing dividend per share) — below 1.0× means the payout is not being funded from free cash flow this year. 73 of 361 measurable names sit below 1.0×, and 49 pay out more than they earn. Red marks the threshold, amber a caution band.

Read both together, and read them gently. Free cash flow is lumpy — a single year of heavy capex or a legal settlement drops coverage without the dividend being at risk, and a writedown inflates the payout ratio without cash changing hands. A name failing one metric warrants a look at the filings; failing both, with revenue declining, is the combination that has historically preceded cuts. 38 names have no coverage figure available and are excluded whenever the FCF filter is above zero.

What this still cannot see: debt maturities, earnings quality, or whether the growth rate is intact. A 30-year dividend CAGR describes the past; the reason to screen on it is that consistency is rare and worth starting from, not that it predicts. Run the dividend-growth columns against each other — a 5-year rate far below the life rate is deceleration the price has usually already noticed.
