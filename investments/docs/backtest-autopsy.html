Option Omega · SPX / QQQ · Sept 2026

Backtest Autopsy

Four checkboxes were inflating every backtest in the book. Finding them cost the iron condors their place in the portfolio — and turned up a sizing bug worth $90,964.

Scope 2020-05-18 → 2026-09-04 Live reference 2026 YTD, 1,879 legs Backtests reviewed 11

A live account returned $22,015 over a period the backtest said was worth $89,666. The gap was not execution, not margin, and not bad luck — it was four Option Omega settings that let the model skip stop-outs the market would have enforced.

Correcting them turned the iron condor book from +$281,047 to −$31,987 across 4.3 years, with a 53% drawdown and an early termination. The ICs came out. What replaced them is a four-strategy book that has now been positive in every calendar year since 2020.

The settings

Four flags that were paying you in fictional dollars

Two of these carry Option Omega's own warning triangle. Check them first on any strategy that uses a stop — they are worth more than any parameter you can tune.

⚠ Ignore Single Bar Stop Loss Breach
ON→OFF
Disregards a stop breach that reverts inside one bar. 92 short legs — 10% of all expired short legs — traded past −85% MAE and still logged as “Expired.” Median excursion on them: −103%. Your broker extends no such courtesy.
⚠ Cap Non-Opening Stop Outs at User-Defined Stop Amount
ON→OFF
Caps the loss at exactly the stop amount even when price blew straight through it. Real stop-outs slip: average stop loss went from −$302 to −$396 once this was off.
Use 0-DTE Intra-Minute Stops
OFF→ON
Off means stops are evaluated only on minute boundaries. All 2,440 timestamps in the export ended in :00 — the live log had sub-second fills. A spike that blows through your stop and reverts within 60 seconds is invisible to the model and fatal to you.
Ignore Trades with Wide Bid-Ask Spread
ON→OFF
Silently declines to enter the hardest-to-fill trades — precisely the population where live fills came in 16% under model on $4+ credit entries. Selection bias in the backtest's favor.
Exit Slippage
blank→0.2
Entry slippage and stop-loss slippage were both set to 0.2. Exit slippage was left empty.

Where both systems agreed, the live account did better — smaller losses on the shared stop-outs, slightly more on the shared expirations. The entire gap sits in one row: 75 trades the model rode to a worthless expiration and the live stop closed at a loss. Realized stop rates were 22.1% modeled against 30.3% live.

Entry slippage across all 526 matched pairs totalled $5,462. Peak margin requirement was $33,096 against an $80,000 account. Neither theory survived contact with the data. The original hypothesis was insufficient margin on high-volatility days

What the live account actually did well

On VIX 22–30 days the live book captured 97% of the backtest ($11,492 against $11,841). Discretionary exits — 88 legs, +$25,517, averaging +$290 per leg — were the single best-performing close reason in the entire book. The damage was concentrated in the quiet-to-mid regime, where the model's forgiveness flags did the most work.

The fully-corrected run carried a 53% max drawdown and terminated early on 2026-07-29 — it ran the account from a $100,798 peak to $50,235 and could not continue. Reality sat between runs two and three: live CIC strategies returned +$7,847 in 2026 against +$16,214 and −$16,321 modeled.

The filter that looked like a rescue

Screening for entries above $9 credit produced +$42,118 across 4.3 years. Of which 2024 alone contributed +$42,103. Every other year netted about fifteen dollars, and 2025 swung from −$30,435 to +$7,506 to −$5,520 as the threshold rose — noise, not signal.

What the long wings were really doing

The wings did explode, exactly as remembered — 38 entries, one of them worth $12,650 on a single leg, netting +$86,412. Carrying them on the other 2,384 entries cost −$185,242. Forty-seven cents back per dollar of insurance. And in the one IC configuration that was actually profitable, the wings paid off zero times out of 1,072.

An iron condor makes you buy wings on both sides, every day, unconditionally. The Long Put Hedge is the same convexity bet placed only when conditions warrant it — 316 conditional entries netting +$43,668 against 2,422 unconditional ones netting −$98,830. Why the structure was wrong for the job

The sizing bug

A denominator that could collapse

Percentage-of-portfolio sizing divides an allocation by margin-per-contract. When a calendar's two legs price nearly identically, that denominator falls toward zero and the contract count explodes.

Seven trades — 0.66% of the strategy — cost $90,964. The largest single position was 1,402 contracts at $10 of margin each, losing $29,162. It was not a real trade at any price.

Minimum premium beats a contract cap

A $0.25 minimum debit removes those seven trades and nothing else. The strategy goes from +$70,719 to +$161,684, and maximum contracts drop from 1,402 to 56 as a side effect — the giant sizes only ever occurred on the degenerate trades, so the cap comes free.

Results are identical from $0.25 through $1.00 and within 3% out to $2.00. That flat plateau with a sharp cliff at the structural break is what a real effect looks like, as against the IC credit filter that wobbled at every threshold. Set it at $1.00 — same result, more buffer, free.

Five percent strictly dominates the two-contract version — higher return and better risk on every single measure. That almost never happens, and it means the original cap was simply mis-set rather than conservative.

Seven percent was tested and rejected

On a matched 2022–2026 window, 7% returned 48.77x against 5%'s 45.22x — roughly 8% more terminal equity — for 2.5 more points of drawdown and a worst day of −8.71% against −6.43%. Sharpe went 2.55 → 2.54 and Sortino 5.46 → 5.49: flat. No efficiency gain, just a slide along the risk axis.

The year-by-year split settles it. At 7% the bear year weakens (+27.6% against +33.9%) while the trend years strengthen and 2025's drawdown deepens to −19.6%. That is the long-beta drift from the uncapped run reappearing in milder form — the wrong trade for a 4-point CAGR pickup when 2022 resilience is the whole point.

Uncapping breaks the property that makes this book unusual. QQQ LEAPs are the only long-delta component in an otherwise vol-neutral or short-delta portfolio; scaled up, they turn the whole thing long-beta and 2022 goes from +36.9% to −4.7%. Realized P/L that year was still +$263,738 — the loss was entirely unrealized marks on LEAPs held through the bear market. The economics survived. The equity curve did not.

Still open

What to decide, and what to simply know

  • Settled

    The flags are off — breach rate is 0.0%. Daily Calendar 14/16 is the only strategy using stops, and zero of its 738 non-stopped legs went past the −30% trigger. Earlier readings of 4.3% and 6.3% were artifacts of pooling all four strategies against one inferred trigger; the other three carry no stop at all. Re-check any future export with mae_check.py, which groups by strategy and isolates short legs.

  • Settled

    QQQ sizing is fixed at 5%. Seven percent was run and rejected: flat Sharpe and Sortino, 2.5 more points of drawdown, and a weaker 2022. The frontier between 5% and 7% is flat, so the conservative point wins. No further tuning — chasing the third decimal is how the IC credit filter turned into 2024 in disguise.

  • Know

    Concentration is permanent. The top 10 legs carry 67% of P/L. Strip the five biggest hedge days and 4.3 years returns 43% of the backtest figure. A stretch with no violent sessions produces roughly a third of what the model shows — that is convexity, not a defect.

  • Know

    2021 is the preview to brace for, not 2022. Calm grinding markets are this book's worst environment: the hedge bled $52,430 that year and the calendars had nothing to work with. The crash years are when it earns.

  • Know

    A liquidity ceiling exists ahead. At 2026 sizing rates the book calls for roughly 29 Daily Calendar and 31 hedge contracts per $1M of equity. Somewhere past $10M, fill depth rather than risk becomes the binding constraint — better to pick that ceiling deliberately than discover it in a bad fill.
