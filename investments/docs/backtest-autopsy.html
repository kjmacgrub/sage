Option Omega · SPX / QQQ · Sept 2026

Backtest Autopsy

Four checkboxes were inflating every backtest in the book. Finding them cost the iron condors their place in the portfolio and turned up a sizing bug worth $90,964. The book has since been rebuilt around the findings — different strategies, different exits, different measurement.

Audit Sept 2026 Book rebuilt Sept 2026 Backtests reviewed 40+

A live account returned $22,015 over a period the backtest said was worth $89,666. The gap was not execution, not margin, and not bad luck — it was four Option Omega settings that let the model skip stop-outs the market would have enforced.

Correcting them turned the iron condor book from +$281,047 to −$31,987 across 4.3 years, with a 53% drawdown and an early termination. The ICs came out.

The sections through Reading a backtest are the audit as it happened and remain accurate. Everything after describes the book as it stands now — the original launch configuration did not survive the work that followed. See Where it landed.

The settings

Four flags that were paying you in fictional dollars

Two of these carry Option Omega's own warning triangle. Check them first on any strategy that uses a stop — they are worth more than any parameter you can tune.

⚠ Ignore Single Bar Stop Loss Breach
ON→OFF
Disregards a stop breach that reverts inside one bar. 92 short legs — 10% of all expired short legs — traded past −85% MAE and still logged as “Expired.” Median excursion on them: −103%. Your broker extends no such courtesy.
⚠ Cap Non-Opening Stop Outs at User-Defined Stop Amount
ON→OFF
Caps the loss at exactly the stop amount even when price blew straight through it. Real stop-outs slip: average stop loss went from −$302 to −$396 once this was off.
Use 0-DTE Intra-Minute Stops
OFF→ON
Off means stops are evaluated only on minute boundaries. All 2,440 timestamps in the export ended in :00 — the live log had sub-second fills. A spike that blows through your stop and reverts within 60 seconds is invisible to the model and fatal to you.
Ignore Trades with Wide Bid-Ask Spread
ON→OFF
Silently declines to enter the hardest-to-fill trades — precisely the population where live fills came in 16% under model on $4+ credit entries. Selection bias in the backtest's favor.
Exit Slippage
blank→0.2
Entry slippage and stop-loss slippage were both set to 0.2. Exit slippage was left empty.

What the live account actually did well

On VIX 22–30 days the live book captured 97% of the backtest ($11,492 against $11,841). Discretionary exits — 88 legs, +$25,517, averaging +$290 per leg — were the single best-performing close reason in the entire book. The damage was concentrated in the quiet-to-mid regime, where the model's forgiveness flags did the most work.

The filter that looked like a rescue

Screening for entries above $9 credit produced +$42,118 across 4.3 years. Of which 2024 alone contributed +$42,103. Every other year netted about fifteen dollars, and 2025 swung from −$30,435 to +$7,506 to −$5,520 as the threshold rose — noise, not signal.

What the long wings were really doing

The wings did explode, exactly as remembered — 38 entries, one of them worth $12,650 on a single leg, netting +$86,412. Carrying them on the other 2,384 entries cost −$185,242. Forty-seven cents back per dollar of insurance. And in the one IC configuration that was actually profitable, the wings paid off zero times out of 1,072.

An iron condor makes you buy wings on both sides, every day, unconditionally. The Long Put Hedge is the same convexity bet placed only when conditions warrant it — 316 conditional entries netting +$43,668 against 2,422 unconditional ones netting −$98,830. Why the structure was wrong for the job

The sizing bug

A denominator that could collapse

Percentage-of-portfolio sizing divides an allocation by margin-per-contract. When a calendar's two legs price nearly identically, that denominator falls toward zero and the contract count explodes.

Seven trades — 0.66% of the strategy — cost $90,964. The largest single position was 1,402 contracts at $10 of margin each, losing $29,162. It was not a real trade at any price.

Minimum premium beats a contract cap

A $0.25 minimum debit removes those seven trades and nothing else. The strategy goes from +$70,719 to +$161,684, and maximum contracts drop from 1,402 to 56 as a side effect — the giant sizes only ever occurred on the degenerate trades, so the cap comes free.

Results are identical from $0.25 through $1.00 and within 3% out to $2.00. That flat plateau with a sharp cliff at the structural break is what a real effect looks like, as against the IC credit filter that wobbled at every threshold. Set it at $1.00 — same result, more buffer, free.

Reading a backtest

Two ways the tester will mislead you

The pre-2022 SPX data is real — but one strategy wasn't

SPX added Tuesday expirations on 18 April 2022 and Thursday on 11 May 2022; before that it listed Monday, Wednesday and Friday only. Every Tuesday/Thursday expiration in the pre-2022 logs checks out: 18 are month-end series, and the rest are holiday shifts — Labor Day, MLK, Presidents Day, Good Friday, Christmas. Option Omega's expiration calendar is accurate.

But nearest-leg DTE at entry tells a different story per strategy. Both calendars target 14 and 2 DTE and are 100% unaffected across the transition. The Long Put Hedge was 62% 0DTE / 38% 1DTE before May 2022, and 100% 0DTE after — nearly two-fifths of its early history is a different instrument, with overnight gap risk and none of the terminal convexity that is the reason to own it. Judge the hedge on post-2022-05-11 data only.

A standalone backtest of one strategy can starve itself

Run alone, QQQ 360's only growth engine is QQQ 360. Equity sits at $82–104k, so a 5% allocation stays near $4,100 while the call costs $3,500–6,800 — and the run skipped 24 of its own 34 signals because it couldn't afford a single contract. Worse, option cost rises with implied volatility, so the entries it declined were the high-IV ones: the deep-bear trades that are the strategy's best.

In the portfolio the other three strategies grow the base to millions, so the same 5% scales from 1 contract to 14. A standalone run is fine for confirming entry and exit logic and close to worthless for judging returns, sizing, or concurrency.

Position sizing

The mechanic behind every sizing decision

Option Omega buys floor(allocation ÷ cost per contract). When that rounds to zero, the trade is skipped entirely — there is no partial fill and no warning in the log. Margin equals premium for a long call; Margin Req. is the total across contracts while Premium is per contract, which is worth remembering before reading a ratio between them as a multiplier.

A 360-DTE 60-delta QQQ call costs a remarkably stable 11–12% of spot notional — median 12.3% across six years. That single ratio is what lets you project forward: at QQQ $718.96 the call runs roughly $7,800–8,600, and a volatility spike pushes it past $10,000. Recheck it before each sizing decision — it tracks spot, not the calendar.

Seven percent was tested and rejected

On a matched 2022–2026 window, 7% returned 48.77x against 5%'s 45.22x — roughly 8% more terminal equity — for 2.5 more points of drawdown and a worst day of −8.71% against −6.43%. Sharpe went 2.55 → 2.54 and Sortino 5.46 → 5.49: flat. No efficiency gain, just a slide along the risk axis.

The year-by-year split settles it. At 7% the bear year weakens (+27.6% against +33.9%) while the trend years strengthen and 2025's drawdown deepens to −19.6%. That is the long-beta drift from the uncapped run reappearing in milder form — the wrong trade for a 4-point CAGR pickup when 2022 resilience is the whole point.

Uncapping breaks the property that makes this book unusual. QQQ LEAPs are the only long-delta component in an otherwise vol-neutral or short-delta portfolio; scaled up, they turn the whole thing long-beta and 2022 goes from +36.9% to −4.7%. Realized P/L that year was still +$263,738 — the loss was entirely unrealized marks on LEAPs held through the bear market. The economics survived. The equity curve did not.

Then QQQ kept going, and the threshold moved again

All of the above was settled when QQQ traded near $280 and a call cost $4,100 — comfortably inside 5% of an $80,000 account. QQQ closed at $718.96 and the call now costs about $7,800. The strategy buys whole contracts, so the allocation is binary: one or none.

The cliff is the constraint, not the cost. At $200k both 5% and 7% buy exactly one contract today — identical positions. What differs is how far the sleeve can fall before the allocation drops under the contract price and the strategy switches itself off. At 5% that is a 22% drawdown; at 7% it is 44%. Since the backtest's own max drawdown is 11%, 5% leaves you switching off the stabiliser during precisely the event it exists for.

And every allocation that fires carries a 30% drawdown

At 12% the strategy trades from day one — and reaches 132% of equity across 8 positions during the April 2025 selloff, producing a −37.0% portfolio drawdown. Adding Max Allocation $25,000 pins position size at a $24,970 maximum and rescues the later years (2025 goes −37.0% → −14.1%), but the cap does not bind until equity passes ~$208,000. 2022 and 2023 come out identical to uncapped: −29.9% and −28.8%.

The early-period concentration isn't caused by the allocation percentage — it's that ten concurrent positions at the minimum possible size is most of a small account. Concurrency is the mechanism: QQQ Leap stacks to ten because a 78-day median hold against a 14-day entry cadence makes it arithmetically unavoidable, and every one of its six lifetime losses came from a single stack entered January–April 2022 as QQQ fell from $373 to $275.

At $80,000 this strategy could not be sized safely. Not at 5%, where it never fires; not at 12%, where it runs a 30% drawdown through the first two years. The account had to grow into it — and at $200,000 it does, at 7%. Why the sleeve needed $200k before this book was viable

Two measurement corrections that changed the numbers

The trade log understates drawdown by 2–3×. Funds at Close only updates when a trade closes, so unrealized losses on open positions never appear. Measured from the daily Net Liquidity export instead, max drawdown is 11.01%, not 3.6%, and time underwater is 71% rather than ~50%. Always export the portfolio CSV alongside the trade log.

Contribution percentage is circular in a compounding book. QQQ Leap showed 3.6% of P/L, which reads like something you could drop. Re-running without it — sizing re-derived, not P/L subtracted — ended at $5.5M instead of $19.8M. Percentage sizing feeds every dollar a strategy earns into every other strategy's position size, permanently. Never judge a strategy by its share; re-run without it.

Portfolio P/L surviving: 5¢ → 78%, 10¢ → 57%, 15¢ → 35%, 25¢ → negative. And that is a linear subtraction, so it understates — losses compound down through sizing.

Under realistic friction the book converges to QQQ Leap plus noise. At 15¢ it is 58% of all P/L; at 25¢ it is the only strategy still positive. The three strategies with thousands of trades are the fragile ones; the robust one has 74 trades and 6 losses.

Measured against 583 matched live-versus-backtest leg-groups, entry slippage runs 4.5–10¢ per leg — comfortably inside the band, at one contract. The median leg fills at or better than the model; the cost sits entirely in an upper quartile giving up $47+ per contract. At 100 contracts it will be worse.

Three sleeves and a ratchet

Why most of the capacity analysis stopped applying

The options book is roughly one third of a whole: options (taxable) · CEFs (Roth) · managed index (taxable), rebalanced about annually. That single fact removes most of the liquidity problem, because the sleeve then grows at the total portfolio's rate, not its own — at a 30% options return that is 15.7%/yr, so $200k reaches about $857,000 at year 10 rather than $35M.

At an $857k sleeve the hedge runs ~30 contracts and QQQ Leap ~14. The 833-contract and 1,850-contract figures that drove the whole liquidity investigation were artifacts of a backtest compounding unchecked.

The rebalance is a ratchet

Profits out on gains, never added back on losses. Classical rebalancing buys the loser on an assumption of mean reversion — the asset is cheap, its long-run return intact. That assumption does not hold for a strategy. An options book underperforming is evidence the edge may have stopped working, not a discount.

It costs about 4 points of portfolio CAGR at a 30% options return — 15.7% against 19.9% un-rebalanced, or $2.57M against $3.67M over ten years. That is the premium for not being concentrated in something unproven, and Section 1256 mark-to-market makes it free at the margin: gains are taxed annually whether the money moves or not.

QQQ Leap still needs rechecking on a schedule

Its minimum position is fixed in dollars while everything else scales by percentage. At QQQ $718.96 a contract runs about $7,800, so 5% needs roughly $150,000 of sleeve and 7% about $111,000. The figure tracks 11–12% of QQQ spot, so it moves with the underlying rather than the calendar — recheck before each step.

The open item: QQQ Leap went from 1–4% of P/L to 20.9% while uncapped, and it is long-dated QQQ rather than 0 DTE SPX. The depth argument that justifies leaving the hedge uncapped does not transfer. Pull open interest on a ~1-year call before leaving it that way.

Tripwires

Decided before the money was at stake

The ratchet is a policy and needs no diagnosis — below target weight, don't top up. These are a diagnosis, and they trigger something more serious: stop adding entirely, or reduce. All are measured against the options sleeve's own backtested behaviour, never against the other two sleeves — relative comparison conflates "the strategies broke" with "equities had a great year," which is the entire point of holding something uncorrelated.

Carry these

Settled questions, and things that are shape rather than defect

  • Gap

    mae_check.py no longer covers this book. Exits are now Greek- and volatility-based — Below Delta, Above Delta, Below Short/Long Ratio, VIX Move Down, Expired, Profit Target. No strategy uses a stop loss, so the checker reports "no stops in use" for all four and cannot run. That is not a clean bill of health; it is an unverified backtest. Risk is still structurally bounded — every strategy is a defined-risk multi-leg structure — but the tool that caught the $22k-live/$89k-backtest gap has no purchase here. Needs a replacement check.

  • Settled

    Don't tune the QQQ entry filter. Every 2022 loser was bought within 7.2% of the prior high and every winner more than 13% below it — the pattern is real, but the cheapest filter that removes those six losses gives up $723,000 of winners to save $23,931. Max RSI 69 also doesn't do what it looks like: RSI is low in a bear market, so the cap only blocks overbought entries.

  • Corrected

    Max Open Positions does survive the portfolio tester. Earlier finding — 5 versus 10 producing byte-identical output — no longer reproduces. The put seller's 5-position cap held in a portfolio run with a time-at-cap distribution nearly identical to standalone (9.4% against 9.2%). Either OO fixed it or the original test hit a different setting. Designs were being built around a constraint that may not exist; worth re-testing deliberately rather than trusting either result.

  • Know

    Concurrency is emergent unless something caps it. The SPX strategies peak at 2 concurrent, but that is not a setting — it falls out of 0–2 day holds against a 2–3 day entry cadence. QQQ Leap peaks at 10 for the same reason in reverse: a 78-day median hold against a 14-day cadence makes stacking unavoidable. Anything that lengthens holds removes the accidental protection — widening the calendar profit target, which tests well in isolation, does exactly that.

  • Know

    Don't pause QQQ when positions stack. The stacking signal is the entry-quality signal. In 2022 every loser came from the first five entries taken with nothing stacked; the best trade of the year was bought on 2 December with fourteen already open. Pausing at three open costs 42% of the strategy.

  • Know

    Concentration is permanent. Nineteen legs carry 60% of P/L. A stretch with no violent sessions produces a fraction of what the model shows — that is convexity, not a defect.

  • Know

    A calm grind is the weak regime, not a crash. 2021 was the worst year in the long run: the hedge bled $52,430 and the calendars had nothing to work with. The bear years are when this book earns.

  • Know

    Liquidity is per-order and per-tenor, not per-strategy. A real chain at SPX ~7,700 showed the 57-DTE weekly Friday carrying 0–8 contracts of put open interest while the 70-DTE monthly carried 77–450, and 8,778 at the round strike. Two consequences: a vertical is limited by its thinner leg, so selling the 7600 and buying the 7595 caps you at 125 regardless of the 8,778; and width buys size — the same dollar risk needs 1,850 contracts of a 5-wide or 370 of a 25-wide, landing on a leg with 8× the depth.

  • Know

    The exposure is premium richness, not 0 DTE depth. Exchanges don't delist revenue and the direction has only gone one way — monthlies, weeklies, Mon/Wed in 2016, Tue/Thu in 2022. What can change is flow, and three of four strategies are net short premium (the hedge is a net credit structure; 197 of its winners keep it by expiring). If the seller population thins, skew reprices and three strategies earn less with nothing visibly breaking. Tested for it: only the MTW calendar shows decay — win rate 58–71% through 2024, then 54.6% and 51.6%. Two years, not a nine-year slope. Watch the 2027 number.

  • Trap

    A conditional distribution cannot tell you what changing the condition does. Hit three times: Max Profit is censored by the exit being assessed, so it can't evaluate a wider profit target; exit-reason P/L is negative because that bucket collects the losers, so tightening the put stop from 90 to 70/80 tested worse, not better; and subtracting a strategy's P/L leaves the other trades at their inflated contract counts, understating QQQ's value fivefold. Only a re-run with the condition changed answers the question.

  • Know

    This book wants room. A wider profit target beat a narrower one; the deep put stop (90) beat the shallow ones; and the hedge's every dollar comes from letting positions expire while its two management exits lose $4.66M combined. Defined-risk structures recover — cutting early converts recoveries into realized losses.
